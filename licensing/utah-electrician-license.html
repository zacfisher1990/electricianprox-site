How to Get Your Electrician License in Utah — A Step-by-Step Guide

Everything you need to know about earning your Utah electrician license — from your first day as an apprentice all the way to your master electrician and electrical contractor license. Hour requirements, exam structure, fees, and realistic timelines, all in one place.

Getting your electrician license in Utah takes time — typically 6–8 years from your first day on the job to legally operating your own electrical contracting business. But the path is straightforward once you understand the choices available to you at each stage.

Utah offers two separate licensing tracks: an unlimited track (Journeyman E200 → Master E400) that covers all electrical work, and a residential track (Residential Journeyman E210 → Residential Master E300) limited to one and two-family homes. Both tracks end at the same place — an Electrical Contractor license (S280) — but the unlimited track opens the door to commercial and industrial work.

Within either track, there are also two ways to meet the hour requirements: completing a DOPL-approved apprenticeship program, or logging on-the-job hours without a formal program. The school path requires significantly fewer hours. All licensing is handled by the Utah Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing (DOPL).

Important Note

Licensing requirements, fees, and procedures can change. This guide reflects requirements as understood in mid-2026, and includes information provided by licensed Utah electricians. Always verify current requirements directly with Utah DOPL at dopl.utah.gov before submitting any application.

Step 1 — Register as an Apprentice Electrician (E100)

The apprentice registration is your entry point regardless of which track you ultimately pursue. It's not a full license — it's a legal registration that allows you to work on electrical systems under the direct, on-site supervision of a licensed journeyman or master electrician. Both the unlimited and residential tracks start here.

Consider a Formal Apprenticeship Program

Joining a DOPL-approved apprenticeship program — through the IBEW/NECA Joint Apprenticeship and Training Committee (JATC) or the Independent Electrical Contractors (IEC) — cuts your required hours in half compared to working without a program. It also includes structured classroom training and is widely recognized by employers. Utah has active JATC programs in Salt Lake City, Ogden, and St. George.

What you can and can't do as an apprentice

As a registered apprentice, you can perform electrical work on job sites — rough-in wiring, conduit installation, pulling wire, installing devices and fixtures — as long as a licensed journeyman or master is on-site supervising your work.

You cannot work independently, pull permits in your own name, or supervise other workers. Every job must have a licensed electrician responsible for the work.

Step 2 — Log Your Hours: 8,000 With School or 16,000 Without

2
Stage Two · Both Tracks
On-the-Job Experience — Two Paths to Journeyman
4–8 years depending on path

This is the longest stage of the licensing path, and one of the most important decisions you'll make early on: whether to complete a DOPL-approved apprenticeship program or accumulate hours independently. The difference is significant — the school path requires half as many hours.

The two paths to a Journeyman Electrician (E200) license

With an Approved Program
8,000 hours
Complete a DOPL-approved apprenticeship (JATC, IEC, or other approved program) alongside supervised on-the-job work. Approximately 4 years full-time.
Without a Formal Program
16,000 hours
Pure on-the-job experience under licensed supervision, no formal apprenticeship program. Approximately 8 years full-time — twice the time.

The same two-path structure applies to the Residential Journeyman (E210), though the specific hour counts differ — approximately 4,000 hours with an approved program or 8,000 hours without. Verify the current E210 requirements with DOPL directly.

Making the most of your experience hours

Not all hours produce equal results. Electricians who work across different project types — residential rough-in, commercial service work, panel upgrades, industrial installations — come out of their apprenticeship with a much broader skill set and exam readiness than those who do one type of work for years on end. If you have any say in the jobs you work, seek variety.

A rough breakdown of what you'll be developing across the timeline:

  • Year 1–2: Foundational skills — rough-in wiring, conduit bending and installation, wire pulling, device installation, safety practices
  • Year 2–3: Panel work, service entrances, 3-phase systems, commercial rough-in, reading and applying code
  • Year 3–4: Complex circuits, motor controls, troubleshooting, load calculations, NEC code application
  • Year 5–8 (no-school path only): Continued depth and breadth, increasingly complex systems

Documenting your hours — start on day one

When you apply for your journeyman license, DOPL requires signed verification of your hours from your employers. This sounds simple until you realize you might be working for multiple employers over 4–8 years, and tracking down a supervisor from five years ago to sign paperwork is not unusual. Keep a running log with dates, employer name, and hours per week from your very first day. A simple spreadsheet is all you need — it could save you months of delay at application time.

Formal Program Participants

If you're in a JATC or IEC program, your program coordinator tracks your hours and handles much of the documentation. These programs also include required classroom instruction — typically around 144 hours per year. Completion of the program typically satisfies the school-path hour requirements, and the documentation burden is significantly lighter at application time.

Step 3 — Journeyman Electrician License (E200)

3

Journeyman Electrician License (E200)

With your documented hours complete, you're eligible to apply for the Journeyman Electrician license (E200). This is one of the most significant milestones in an electrician's career — a journeyman license allows you to work independently on any electrical work in Utah, supervise apprentices, and operate without direct oversight on every task.

Many electricians hold the E200 for their entire career. It's only those aiming to run their own business or move into supervisory project roles who pursue the master license. The E200 is a fully capable, respected license on its own.

The 3-part Journeyman exam

The Utah Journeyman Electrician exam is not a single test — it consists of three separate parts, each testing a different dimension of your electrical knowledge. You must pass all three parts to receive your license.

1
Practical
A hands-on skills assessment covering the core physical tasks of electrical work — wiring, terminations, conduit work, reading diagrams, and demonstrating safe work practices. Tests what you can actually do, not just what you know on paper.
2
Theory
Covers electrical fundamentals — Ohm's Law, circuit analysis, single and three-phase power, motors, transformers, and system design concepts. Tests your understanding of how electricity works and why, not just what the code says to do.
3
Code (Open Book)
Covers the National Electrical Code (NEC) and Utah state amendments. This is an open-book exam — you bring your codebook. The test requires you to navigate the NEC quickly and accurately to answer code compliance questions. Knowing where to look is as important as knowing the rules.

How to prepare for all three parts

Each part requires different preparation and most people benefit from treating them as three separate study targets:

  • Practical: This should come from your years on the job, but refresh yourself on specific skills that may be tested — neat conduit bends, proper terminations, panel wiring. If your employer will let you practice specific skills before the test, do it.
  • Theory: Study systematically. Electrical theory builds on itself — make sure your fundamentals (voltage, current, resistance, power) are solid before tackling more complex topics. Prep books and online courses help structure this.
  • Code: Tab your NEC codebook before the exam. Know where to find the key tables — 310.16 (ampacity), Chapter 9 (conduit fill), Article 220 (load calculations), Article 250 (grounding). Speed matters as much as accuracy in the code section.

A passing score is typically 75% on each part. Most candidates find the theory section the most challenging if they haven't actively studied it. Don't assume years of field experience translates directly to exam performance — the exam tests code knowledge and theory specifically.

The Residential Track — E210 & E300

If your goal is to work exclusively in residential electrical — new home construction, remodels, service work on houses — the residential track (E210 → E300) is a separate and valid path. It has lower hour requirements and a narrower exam scope than the unlimited track, in exchange for a restricted license that covers only one and two-family residential work.

The residential track still leads to an Electrical Contractor license (S280), which means you can legally run your own residential electrical contracting business. The tradeoff is that any commercial or multi-family work would be outside your scope.

The E300 exam has two parts — Theory and Code — with no practical component, consistent with the master-level exams on the unlimited track. Content covers residential system design, service sizing for homes, residential load calculations, and NEC compliance for dwelling units.

Scope Limitation

The E300 license qualifies you as a master only for residential contracting work. If you obtain an electrical contractor license (S280) with an E300 as your qualifier, your contracting license is limited to residential scope. To bid commercial work, you need the E400. Some residential contractors later pursue the E400 to expand into light commercial work — this is allowed and common.

Step 4 — Gain Journeyman Experience Before the Master Exam

4
Stage Four · Unlimited Track
Work ~2 Years as a Licensed Journeyman (E200)
~2 years

After earning your E200, you'll need to work as a licensed journeyman for approximately two years before you're eligible to apply for the master electrician exam. This requirement exists because the master exam covers system design, complex load calculations, and supervisory responsibilities that assume a level of experience beyond what most people have right out of the journeyman exam.

Use this time intentionally if running your own business is the goal

  • Get exposure to the business side. If your employer will let you shadow on estimates, sit in on permit applications, or review job costs, take every opportunity. This knowledge is invaluable when you're writing your own bids.
  • Start studying for the master exam. The master exam is more rigorous than the journeyman, and two years is the right window to start building toward it — especially while you're working full-time.
  • Build your professional network. Suppliers, inspectors, general contractors, and other tradespeople you work with now will become the foundation of your business relationships when you go independent.
  • Save capital. Starting an electrical contracting business requires upfront money — tools, insurance, bond, licensing fees, vehicle, and enough operational runway to survive the first few months before cash flow stabilizes. Plan for it now, not later.

Keep documenting your hours during this stage, just as you did during your apprenticeship. You'll need employer verifications again when you apply for the E400.

The 2-part Master Electrician exam

The master exam drops the practical component — by this stage, your hands-on competence is established. The exam focuses entirely on system design knowledge and code mastery.

1
Theory
Advanced electrical theory — three-phase power systems, transformer sizing and connections, motor circuits and protection, emergency and standby systems, service and feeder design. More complex than the journeyman theory exam, with greater emphasis on system-level thinking and engineering principles.
2
Code (Open Book)
A deeper NEC exam than the journeyman version — emphasizes Articles 220 (load calculations), 230 (services), 240 (overcurrent protection), 250 (grounding), 700–708 (emergency and standby systems), and special occupancies. Open book, but speed and precision navigating the code matter at this level.

Preparing for the master exam

The master exam is a meaningful step up from the journeyman. Many electricians report spending 60–100 hours of dedicated study to prepare for it. The theory section in particular covers material that doesn't come up day-to-day on residential and commercial service work — don't assume field experience alone is enough preparation.

Tab your NEC codebook thoroughly before the exam, focus prep time on the load calculation and service sizing articles, and seriously consider a master exam prep course. The same exam prep providers who offer journeyman prep also typically offer master-level courses.

License Renewal — Critical for Contractors

Utah master electrician licenses must be renewed every two years. Renewal requires continuing education — typically 8 hours per renewal period including code update content. If you hold a contractor license with yourself as the qualifying individual, an expired master license can suspend your contractor license. Track your renewal dates carefully and schedule CE well in advance.

Step 6 — Electrical Contractor License (S280)

6
Stage Six · Both Tracks
Electrical Contractor License (S280)
Business license

The electrical contractor license is what allows you to legally bid, contract, and perform electrical work as a business in Utah. Even if you hold a master electrician license, you cannot legally operate as an independent electrical contracting business without the S280. The contractor license is a business license, entirely separate from your individual master license.

Both the E300 (Residential Master) and the E400 (Master Electrician) can serve as the qualifying license for an S280 — but the scope of work you can legally contract is limited by the qualifier's license scope.

If the qualifier's license lapses or they leave the company, the contractor license can be suspended.

Setting up your business before you apply

Several business setup steps need to happen before or alongside your contractor license application:

  • Choose a business structure. Most electrical contractors form an LLC. It provides liability protection without the administrative overhead of a corporation. An attorney can advise on the right structure for your situation.
  • Get an EIN. Apply for a Federal Employer Identification Number from the IRS — it's free and takes minutes online. You'll need it for banking, taxes, and subcontracting relationships.
  • Open a dedicated business bank account. Never commingle business and personal funds. It complicates taxes and weakens your liability protection.
  • Workers' compensation coverage. Required if you have employees. Even solo, many general contractors require proof of workers' comp before they'll allow you on site.

Local municipality requirements

Beyond your state contractor license, many Utah cities and counties have their own permit and contractor registration requirements. Salt Lake City, Provo, St. George, Ogden, and other municipalities may require you to register at the city level before pulling permits in their jurisdiction. Check with each city's building department before starting work in a new area.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I go the 16,000-hour route (no school), do I get the same E200 license?

Yes — the end result is identical. Whether you earned your E200 via 8,000 hours with an approved program or 16,000 hours without one, you hold the same Journeyman Electrician license with the same scope and rights. The difference is only in how long it took you to qualify. That said, the 16,000-hour path takes roughly twice as long and costs you two to four more years of journeyman earning potential — so the math generally favors the school path if it's accessible to you.

Which track should I choose — unlimited (E200/E400) or residential (E210/E300)?

It depends entirely on what kind of work you want to do long-term. If you're certain you'll work exclusively in residential — new homes, remodels, service work — the residential track gets you to a contractor license faster. But if there's any chance you'll want to bid commercial work, light industrial, or multi-family projects, start on the unlimited track. Many residential electricians later regret not having the E400 when a commercial opportunity comes along. You can always obtain the E400 later, but you'll need to meet all its requirements from scratch.

Does the master exam include a practical component?

Based on information from licensed Utah electricians, the master exam (both E300 and E400) consists of Theory and Code only — there is no practical component at the master level. That said, exam structures can change, so verify the current format with DOPL or PSI before registering for your exam.

Can I skip the journeyman license and go directly to master?

No. Utah's licensing structure is sequential — you must hold the appropriate journeyman license (E200 or E210) and have worked in that role for approximately two years before you're eligible for the corresponding master exam. There is no path that bypasses the journeyman stage.

Can I work as an electrical contractor with just a journeyman license?

No. Operating an electrical contracting business in Utah requires an electrical contractor license (S280), which in turn requires a qualifying master electrician. A journeyman license authorizes you to do the electrical work, but not to legally bid and contract work as a business in your own name.

What if I'm already licensed in another state — can I get reciprocity in Utah?

Utah does not offer formal reciprocity for out-of-state electrical licenses. However, DOPL may consider your documented out-of-state experience toward the hour requirements on a case-by-case basis. Contact DOPL directly early in your process to understand how your experience will be evaluated before you make plans around it.

What does the "qualifying individual" do for the contractor license?

The qualifying master electrician is the individual legally responsible for the quality and code compliance of all work performed by the contracting business. They must hold an active Utah master license and be meaningfully involved in day-to-day operations. If the qualifier leaves the company or lets their individual license lapse, DOPL can suspend the contractor license until a replacement is named.

How long does DOPL take to process applications?

Processing times vary — typically two to six weeks under normal conditions, longer during busy periods. Incomplete applications are the most common cause of delays. Make sure all documentation — especially employer hour verifications — is complete and signed before you submit. Check the DOPL website for current processing time estimates.

Where can I find exam prep courses for the journeyman or master exam?

Several providers offer Utah electrical exam prep, both in-person and online. Community colleges (Salt Lake Community College, Utah Valley University), trade schools, and private prep companies all offer courses covering all three exam parts. Word of mouth from other Utah electricians is often the most reliable guide to which resources are actually effective for the Utah-specific exam format.
