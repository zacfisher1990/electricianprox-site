Getting your electrician license in Utah takes time — typically 6–8 years from your first day on the job to the point where you can legally operate your own electrical contracting business. But the path is straightforward if you understand what's required at each stage and plan accordingly.

All electrician licensing in Utah is managed by the Utah Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing (DOPL). Every license — from apprentice registration to electrical contractor — goes through them. The licensing structure moves in a clear progression: Apprentice → Journeyman → Master Electrician → Electrical Contractor.

This guide walks through each stage in order, with the specific requirements, exam details, fees, and practical advice for each step.

Important Note

Licensing requirements, fees, and procedures can change. This guide reflects the requirements as of mid-2026. Always verify current requirements directly with Utah DOPL at dopl.utah.gov before submitting any application.

How the Utah Electrician Licensing System Works

Utah uses a tiered licensing system administered by DOPL. Each tier builds on the last, and you cannot skip levels — you must hold each license before you can apply for the next. Here's how the four tiers break down:

  • Apprentice Electrician (E100): A registration (not a full license) that allows you to legally work on electrical systems under the direct supervision of a licensed journeyman or master electrician. There is no exam required — just an application and fee.
  • Journeyman Electrician (E200): A full license that lets you work independently on electrical systems. Requires roughly 8,000 hours of on-the-job experience and passing a written exam.
  • Master Electrician (E300/E400): The highest individual license. Lets you design and oversee electrical installations and supervise journeymen and apprentices. Requires additional experience as a journeyman and a more advanced exam.
  • Electrical Contractor (S280): A business license that allows you to legally bid and contract electrical work. Requires a qualifying master electrician and satisfying insurance and bonding requirements.

Note that Utah also has a Residential Master Electrician (E300) license, which is limited to single-family residential work, and a Master Electrician (E400) license for unlimited commercial and residential scope. Most people pursuing a contractor license aim for the E400.

Pro Tip — Join a Formal Apprenticeship Program

While not strictly required by DOPL, completing a formal apprenticeship program significantly improves your outcomes. Programs through the IBEW/NECA Joint Apprenticeship and Training Committee (JATC) or the Independent Electrical Contractors (IEC) combine supervised on-the-job training with classroom instruction and are widely recognized by employers. Utah has active JATC programs in Salt Lake City, Ogden, and St. George.

What you can and can't do as an apprentice

As a registered apprentice, you can perform electrical work on job sites, including wiring, installing devices and fixtures, pulling wire, and most hands-on tasks — as long as a licensed journeyman or master is on-site supervising your work.

You cannot work independently, pull permits in your own name, or supervise other workers. Every job must have a licensed electrician responsible for the work.

Step 2 — Complete Your Apprenticeship

2
Stage Two
Complete 8,000 Hours of On-the-Job Training
~4 years

The journey from apprentice to journeyman requires accumulating 8,000 hours of supervised electrical work experience — approximately four years working full-time. This is the longest stage of the licensing path, and what happens during these hours matters enormously for the rest of your career.

Making the most of your apprenticeship hours

Not all hours are equal. Electricians who work across a variety of project types — residential, commercial, industrial, service work — come out of their apprenticeship with a much deeper knowledge base than those who spent four years doing the same type of work repeatedly. If you have a say in the jobs you work, diversity of experience is worth seeking out.

The 8,000-hour requirement breaks down roughly as follows, though your exact experience will vary:

  • Year 1–2: Foundation skills — rough-in wiring, conduit bending, wire pulling, device installation, basic safety practices
  • Year 2–3: Panel work, service entrance, 3-phase systems, commercial rough-in, code reading
  • Year 3–4: Complex circuits, motor controls, troubleshooting, load calculations, NEC code application

Documenting your hours

Keep a detailed log of your work hours from day one. When you apply for your journeyman license, you'll need to provide signed verification from your employers confirming your hours. Missing or undocumented hours can delay your application significantly — some applicants have had to track down employers from years prior to get signatures. A simple spreadsheet tracking dates, employer, and hours per week costs you nothing and could save you months of headache later.

If You're in a Formal Program

JATC and IEC apprenticeship programs track your hours for you and include the required classroom instruction (typically 144 hours per year). If you're in one of these programs, your program coordinator handles most of the documentation. Completion of a five-year JATC program typically satisfies or exceeds the 8,000-hour requirement for journeyman licensure.

Step 3 — Get Your Journeyman Electrician License

3
Stage Three
Journeyman Electrician License (E200)
Exam required

With 8,000 hours of documented experience, you're eligible to apply for your Journeyman Electrician license (E200). This is a major milestone — a journeyman license allows you to work independently, take on service calls solo, and supervise apprentices. Most electricians hold this license for their entire career; it's only those who want to run their own business or take on supervisory roles who pursue the master.

The Journeyman exam

The Utah Journeyman Electrician exam is an open-book test — you're allowed to bring your codebook. This doesn't make it easy; it makes knowing how to navigate the NEC quickly and accurately more important than memorization. The exam is administered by PSI Services at testing centers around Utah (Salt Lake City, Ogden, St. George, and others). You schedule directly through PSI after applying to DOPL.

The exam covers:

  • NEC wiring methods and materials
  • Branch circuit and feeder calculations
  • Service entrance requirements
  • Grounding and bonding
  • Motor and HVAC circuits
  • Special occupancies
  • Utah state amendments to the NEC

How to prepare for the exam

Most people underestimate the journeyman exam on their first attempt. A passing score is typically 75%, and real-world electrical experience, while helpful, doesn't directly translate to exam performance. The exam tests code knowledge specifically — not field technique.

Recommended preparation:

  • Get the current NEC codebook and tab it well before the exam. Know where to find key tables — 310.16 (ampacity), Chapter 9 (conduit fill), Article 220 (load calculations).
  • Take a journeyman prep course. Electrician exam prep courses, available in person and online, are worth the investment. They teach you exam technique, not just electrical theory.
  • Practice timed questions. Four hours sounds like a lot until you're working through 80 code lookups. Timed practice builds the speed you need.
  • Review Utah state amendments. Utah adopts the NEC with some modifications. Know the key differences.

Step 4 — Work as a Licensed Journeyman

4
Stage Four
Gain 2 Years of Journeyman Experience
~2 years

After earning your journeyman license, you'll need to work as a licensed journeyman for approximately two years (4,000 hours) before you're eligible to sit for the master electrician exam. This requirement exists because the master exam assumes a depth of experience beyond what most people have right after their journeyman exam — larger systems, project oversight, complex troubleshooting.

What to focus on during this stage

If running your own business is your goal, use this time intentionally:

  • Seek exposure to the business side. If you're working for a contractor who will let you shadow on estimates, pull permits, or review job costs, take every opportunity. This knowledge is invaluable when you're running your own jobs.
  • Study the master exam material. The master exam is more rigorous than the journeyman exam, and the two-year window is the right time to start building toward it — especially if you're also working full-time.
  • Build your network. Suppliers, inspectors, GCs, and other tradespeople you work with now will become the foundation of your business relationships when you go out on your own.
  • Save money. Starting an electrical contracting business requires upfront capital — tools, insurance, bond, licensing fees, vehicle, and operational runway before cash flow stabilizes. Plan for it now.

Continue tracking your hours meticulously, just as you did during your apprenticeship. You'll need employer verification again when you apply for your master license.

Step 5 — Get Your Master Electrician License

5
Stage Five
Master Electrician License (E300 / E400)
Exam required

The master electrician license is the highest individual electrical license in Utah. It authorizes you to design electrical systems, supervise journeymen and apprentices, and — critically — serve as the qualifying individual for an electrical contractor license.

Choosing between E300 and E400

Utah offers two master electrician licenses:

  • Residential Master Electrician (E300): Limited to single-family and two-family residential work. Easier exam, more limited scope. If you plan to work strictly in residential, this may be sufficient.
  • Master Electrician (E400): Unlimited scope — residential, commercial, and industrial. The exam is more comprehensive. If you want to bid any commercial work or leave your options open, pursue the E400.

The Master Electrician exam

The master exam is a step up from the journeyman exam in scope and difficulty. It covers everything on the journeyman exam plus:

  • Service and feeder design and sizing
  • Complex load calculations (Article 220)
  • Three-phase power systems
  • Emergency and standby systems
  • Electrical plan review and system design concepts
  • Supervision and code compliance responsibilities

The same preparation advice applies as for the journeyman exam — tab your codebook, take a prep course, and practice timed questions. Many electricians report spending 60–100 hours studying for the master exam. Don't underestimate it.

License Renewal

Utah master electrician licenses must be renewed every two years. Renewal requires continuing education — typically 8 hours per renewal period, including code update courses. Track your renewal dates carefully; an expired license can disqualify you as the qualifying individual for your contractor license.

Step 6 — Get Your Electrical Contractor License

6
Stage Six
Electrical Contractor License (S280)
Business license

The electrical contractor license is what allows you to legally bid, contract, and perform electrical work as a business in Utah. Without it, you cannot legally operate as an independent electrical contractor — even if you hold a master electrician license. The contractor license is a business license, separate from and in addition to your individual master license.

Requirements

⚡
Qualifying Master Electrician
The business must have a licensed master electrician serving as the qualifying individual. This can be you (if you hold the E400) or a licensed master you employ. The qualifier is responsible for the quality and code compliance of all work performed by the business.

Setting up your business properly

Before you apply for your contractor license, there are several business setup steps to handle:

  • Choose a business structure. Most electrical contractors operate as an LLC. It provides liability protection without the complexity of a corporation. A business attorney can advise on what's right for your situation, and the filing fee in Utah is relatively modest.
  • Get an EIN. Apply for a Federal Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS. It's free, takes minutes online, and you'll need it for banking, taxes, and subcontracting work.
  • Open a business bank account. Separate your business and personal finances from day one. Commingling makes tax filing harder and weakens your liability protection.
  • Workers' compensation insurance. If you have employees, Utah requires workers' comp coverage. Even if you're solo, some general contractors require it before they'll let you on site.

Local permits and requirements

Beyond your state contractor license, many Utah municipalities have their own permit and registration requirements. Salt Lake City, Provo, St. George, and other cities may require you to register as a contractor at the city level before pulling permits in their jurisdiction. Check with each city's building department before starting work there.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I skip the journeyman license and go straight to master?

No. Utah's licensing structure is sequential. You must hold a journeyman license and have worked as a licensed journeyman for approximately two years before you're eligible to sit for the master exam. There is no path that bypasses the journeyman stage.

Can I work as an electrical contractor with just a journeyman license?

No. To legally bid and contract electrical work in Utah as a business, you need both an electrical contractor license (S280) and a qualified master electrician on the business. A journeyman license allows you to do the work, but not to run a contracting business in your own name.

What if I'm already licensed in another state — can I get reciprocity in Utah?

Utah does not offer formal reciprocity for out-of-state electrician licenses. However, DOPL may consider your documented out-of-state experience toward the hour requirements when you apply. Contact DOPL directly to understand how your experience will be evaluated — the process is handled case by case.

Do I need a separate license for each type of work (residential vs. commercial)?

The E400 Master Electrician license covers both residential and commercial work. If you hold an E300 (Residential Master), you are limited to single-family and two-family residential work. For commercial work, you need the E400.

What does the qualifying master electrician do for a contractor license?

The qualifying master electrician is the individual who is legally responsible for the quality and code compliance of all electrical work performed by the contracting business. They must hold an active Utah master electrician license and be meaningfully involved in the business's operations — DOPL takes this requirement seriously. If the qualifying individual's license lapses or they leave the company, the contractor license can be suspended until a replacement qualifier is named.

How long does DOPL take to process applications?

Processing times vary. Standard applications are often processed within two to six weeks, but this can be longer during busy periods. Check the DOPL website for current processing time estimates, and make sure your application is complete — missing documentation is the most common cause of delays.

Where can I find a prep course for the journeyman or master exam?

Several providers offer Utah electrical exam prep courses, both in-person and online. Trade schools, community colleges (Salt Lake Community College, Utah Valley University), and private exam prep companies all offer courses. Ask other electricians in your area which prep resources they've found most useful — local knowledge is often the best guide.
