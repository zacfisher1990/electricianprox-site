From Trainee to Electrical Contractor in Washington: Every Step and Legal Requirement

Washington runs the whole path through one agency. The Department of Labor & Industries issues the trainee certificate, the electrician certificates, the master and administrator certificates, and the electrical contractor license. That makes it simpler than California or Oregon in one way, and stricter in others: the supervision ratios are written into statute, and as of July 2026 the only way to reach the general journey level certificate is through a registered apprenticeship.

The short answerEveryone learning the trade in Washington needs an electrical training certificate, and trainees work under strict supervision ratios: one trainee per journey level electrician doing journey level work, two per specialty electrician doing specialty work. The general journey level (01) certificate takes 8,000 hours, of which 4,000 must be new industrial or commercial work, plus 96 hours of classroom instruction and an open-book exam scored at 70 percent. Since July 1, 2026, those hours only count inside a registered apprenticeship. To run the business you need an electrical contractor license, which requires a $4,000 bond and a designated master electrician or electrical administrator who is either an owner of the firm or a full-time supervisory employee.

By Zac Fisher, licensed journeyman electrician (Utah and Oregon) · Published September 11, 2026 · Requirements current as of September 2026. This is a guide, not legal advice. Confirm current fees and rules with L&I before you file.

Why Washington

Washington pays electricians well. BLS put the annual mean wage for Washington electricians at $94,470 in May 2025, with a median of $95,220 and 19,380 electricians employed (BLS OEWS). Only Oregon has a higher state mean.

The state's Employment Security Department projects electrician employment growing from 24,635 in 2024 to 26,239 in 2034, with an average of 2,440 total openings a year between 2024 and 2029 (ESD projections). The two agencies count differently, so their employment totals don't match: use BLS for wages and ESD for openings, not both for headcount.

How Washington is different

If you've looked at California or Oregon, three things here will surprise you.

  • One agency, not three. L&I handles the individual certificates and the contractor license. There's no separate contractor board for electrical work.
  • There is a trainee certificate. Oregon has no unlicensed helper route and no trainee card. Washington does, and you can get one at 16.
  • Supervision ratios are in the statute, with percentages attached, and there are penalties for getting them wrong.

One more, and it's recent. The route that let trainees accumulate general journey level hours outside a registered apprenticeship ended on July 1, 2026. In L&I's words: "Only registered apprentices can perform general journey-level (01) electrical work and gain hours of experience in the 01 category. Trainees do not need to be a registered apprentice to perform specialty electrical work" (L&I, Electrical Currents, July 2026). Older guides, and even some text still printed in the rules, describe the old route. It's closed.

Step 1: Get your electrical training certificate

"All apprentices and individuals learning the electrical construction trade shall obtain an electrical training certificate from the department" (RCW 19.28.161). That includes registered apprentices, who also have to carry proof of their program registration.

  • Age: "All applicants for an electrical training certificate must be at least 16 years of age" (WAC 296-46B-942).
  • Term: "The original training certificate will be valid for two years," and after that it renews every two years.
  • Classroom: each renewal requires "forty-eight hours of: Approved classroom training covering this chapter, the national electrical code, or electrical theory" (RCW 19.28.161). The rules add that basic trainee classes "must be classroom instruction only" and that "Correspondence and internet classes are not allowed" (WAC 296-46B-970). Apprenticeship classes count toward the 48 hours.
  • No classes, no renewal: "An individual may not renew a training certificate until the required hours of basic classroom education have been completed."
  • Fees: $56.00 by mail or in person, $48.10 online, with renewals at $67.90 and $59.20 (WAC 296-46B-909).

File your hours on time. Affidavits of experience received more than 180 days after a certificate expiration "shall not count towards hours required to qualify for electrician certification examinations" (WAC 296-46B-942). Hours you don't report on schedule are gone. If you're in an approved apprenticeship, the program can file the affidavits for you at no cost.

Step 2: Work under supervision, and watch the ratios

Washington writes its ratios into statute, and they differ by the kind of work being done (RCW 19.28.161):

  • Journey level work: "not more than one noncertified individual for every certified master journey level electrician or journey level electrician."
  • Specialty work: "not more than two noncertified individuals for every certified master specialty electrician working in that electrician's specialty."
  • Trade school students: the ratio is "one certified … to no more than four students enrolled in and working as part of an electrical construction program."

Two more rules decide what supervision actually means:

  • The 75 percent rule. The certified electrician "shall be on the same jobsite as the noncertified individual for a minimum of seventy-five percent of each working day."
  • Three or four trainees. Then the supervising electrician must "Directly supervise and instruct the noncertified individuals and the certified electrician may not directly make or engage in an electrical installation," and must be on site "one hundred percent of each working day."

Working without direct supervision is possible in narrow cases: during "the last six months of meeting the practical experience requirements," or with a modified supervision certificate, which requires that the trainee "has worked over 7,000 hours properly supervised not to include more than 4,000 of specialty experience" and "has not previously failed a journey level electrician certificate of competency examination" (WAC 296-46B-942).

Getting ratios wrong is an employer penalty, and repeat violations trigger an audit. See the penalties section below.

Step 3: Pick your certificate and build hours

L&I issues the general journey level certificate and fourteen specialties. Washington uses bare parenthetical codes: (01), (02), (06A) and so on, not the "EL01" style you'll see on some websites.

The general journey level (01) requires 8,000 hours, and the composition matters: "Four thousand of the hours must be new industrial or commercial electrical installations under the supervision of a master journey level electrician or journey level electrician and not more than a total of 4,000 hours in all specialties" (RCW 19.28.191). You also need 96 hours of classroom instruction to sit the exam (WAC 296-46B-945).

The specialty hours don't all ladder up. "Electrical construction training hours gained in specialties requiring 2,000 hours or less for certification will not be credited towards qualification for journey level electrician or any of the 4,000 hour specialties." The one exception: the 2,000 hours for the restricted HVAC/refrigeration (06B) specialty count toward the 4,000 for the full (06A).

If you already hold a 4,000-hour specialty certificate and then enter an apprenticeship, "your program is required to credit 4,000 hours for previous experience" (L&I).

Step 4: Pass the exam

Washington's exams are administered by PSI, and they're open book. You need 70 percent on every section (L&I).

  • General journey level (01): 60 questions in 3 hours on the NEC and theory, plus 17 questions in 1 hour on Washington codes.
  • Application fee: $114.60, of which $44.20 is nonrefundable (WAC 296-46B-909).
  • PSI's fee is charged by exam length, from $45.00 for one hour to $105.00 for seven.
  • Retakes: you wait two weeks between attempts. Fail any section three times within your one-year exam period and the wait becomes 90 days. After a year, you retake the whole exam.
  • Challenges must reach the Chief Electrical Inspector "no later than 20 days after your exam was taken."

One thing to verify before you study. Washington's rules adopted the 2023 National Electrical Code, and the 2026 edition replaces it on December 31, 2026 (WAC 296-46B-010). But PSI's candidate bulletin and L&I's exam page both still say exam questions are based on the 2020 NEC. Check which edition your exam actually uses when you apply.

Step 5: Keep the certificate current

  • Term: your first certificate runs at least two and not more than three years, expiring on your birthday. Renewals run three years (WAC 296-46B-940).
  • Fee: $106.40 by mail or in person, $92.60 online.
  • Continuing education: 24 hours per three-year period. "At least eight hours of the total required continuing education must be on the changes in the currently adopted code," and "Four hours of the required continuing education must be on the currently adopted chapter 19.28 RCW and/or its related WAC" (WAC 296-46B-970). No safety hours are required; first aid classes are optional and capped at four hours of credit.
  • Trainee classes don't double count. A basic trainee class used for your training certificate can't also count as continuing education.
  • If you're late: you have 90 days to renew with a late fee. "All applications for renewal received more than ninety days after the expiration date of the certificate of competency require that the electrician pass the appropriate competency examination before being recertified."
  • If you skip the CE: the certificate goes inactive until you complete it.

If you're coming from another state

Washington has exactly one published reciprocity agreement, and it's with Oregon. It covers the general journey level (01) certificate only (L&I).

To use it, you need an Oregon general journeyman (J) license "obtained after completing 8,000-hour Apprenticeships requiring at least 576 classroom hours of education and passing Oregon exams," or an Oregon General Supervising Electrician (S) license obtained by Oregon examination.

The limits are firm:

  • "No one may reciprocate a license that was not obtained through an Oregon examination."
  • "Washington Master (01) General Journey Level Electrician Certificates cannot be obtained through reciprocity."
  • "An individual may only apply for reciprocity from another state(s) one time in Washington" (WAC 296-46B-940).
  • You're disqualified if you've "taken and failed a Washington exam for the certificate being applied for within the past two years."
  • The application fee is $114.60.

If you're coming from anywhere else, there's no reciprocity, but the experience routes in RCW 19.28.191 still apply: an equivalent out-of-state apprenticeship, an out-of-state journey certificate from a state requiring at least 8,000 hours, or "at least 16,000 hours of out-of-state experience in the electrical construction trade." All of them still require 4,000 hours of new industrial or commercial work.

Step 6: Become a master electrician or an administrator

To hold an electrical contractor license, the business has to designate someone. That person is either a master electrician or an electrical administrator, and the two have very different entry requirements.

Master electrician requires time already served: a master journey level applicant must "have possessed a valid journey level electrician certificate of competency for four years," and a master specialty applicant needs two years in that specialty (RCW 19.28.191).

Electrical administrator has no experience prerequisite at all. The rule is explicit: "There are no qualification requirements for taking an administrator certificate examination" (WAC 296-46B-935). It's an exam-only credential, and it's the route many contracting businesses use.

Either way:

  • The certificate "must be renewed every three years, upon application, on or before the holder's birthday" (RCW 19.28.061).
  • Continuing education is 24 hours per three-year cycle, including 8 hours of code update and 4 hours on chapter 19.28 RCW and its rules.
  • The administrator certificate costs $160.00 originally, with renewals at $202.20 by mail or $175.70 online (WAC 296-46B-909).
  • The master exam application is $204.60; the administrator exam application is $44.20.

Step 7: Get the electrical contractor license

Without this license it's "unlawful for any person, firm, partnership, corporation, or other entity to advertise, offer to do work, submit a bid, engage in, conduct, or carry on the business of installing or maintaining wires or equipment to convey electric current" (RCW 19.28.041).

What it takes:

  • A designated master electrician or administrator. The applicant "must designate an individual who currently possesses a valid master journey level electrician's certificate of competency, master specialty electrician's certificate of competency in the specialty for which application has been made, or administrator's certificate."
  • That person works for you, and only you. They must "be a member of the firm or a supervisory employee and shall be available during working hours to carry out the duties," a supervisory employee "shall be a full-time supervisory employee," and "No person may concurrently qualify as master electrician or administrator for more than one contractor" (RCW 19.28.061). If the relationship ends, you notify L&I in writing within ten days.
  • A $4,000 bond. The application "shall be accompanied by a bond in the sum of four thousand dollars with the state of Washington named as obligee." One bond covers multiple specialties.
  • Liability insurance is not required by L&I for this license. L&I says so plainly: "Electrical contractors are encouraged to carry general liability insurance. However, L&I does not keep records of liability insurance for electrical contractors" (L&I). Carry it anyway, but don't expect the state to set the amount.
  • Workers' compensation. The application requires "evidence of workers' compensation coverage for the applicant's employees working in Washington," meaning your L&I industrial insurance account number.
  • A UBI number issued by the Department of Revenue or the Secretary of State.
  • Fees and term: $353.90 by mail, in person or fax, or $306.20 fully online. "All electrical contractor licenses expire twenty-four calendar months following the day of their issue."

License types follow the same codes as the certificates: 01 general electrical, 02 residential, 03 pump and irrigation, 04 signs, 06 limited energy system, 07 nonresidential maintenance, 09 telecommunications, 10 door and gate systems, among others (WAC 296-46B-920).

Do you also need contractor registration? Usually not, if you only do electrical work. The registration chapter lists among its exemptions "an electrician certified under the laws of the state of Washington," and adds that "The exemption provided in this subsection is applicable only when the person certified is operating within the scope of his or her certification" (RCW 18.27.090). L&I puts it plainly: "If an electrical contracting business does other, non-electrical work, registration as a contractor may also be required." If that's you, specialty contractor registration carries a $15,000 bond and general carries $30,000, both raised as of July 1, 2024 (RCW 18.27.040).

Step 8: Set up the business

  • Business license and UBI. Washington requires a business license if "Your gross income is $12,000 per year or more," and the UBI number "registers you with several state agencies." The application costs $50 to open a new business, $10 for most other purposes such as hiring employees or registering a trade name, and $5 a year to renew (Department of Revenue).
  • Entity. An LLC certificate of formation is $180 and the annual report is $70 (Secretary of State).
  • B&O tax. Washington has no corporate income tax. Instead it taxes gross receipts, and "businesses cannot deduct expenses such as labor, materials, taxes, or other costs" (Department of Revenue). Most contractor work falls under Retailing at 0.471 percent. The Service and Other Activities rate became tiered on October 1, 2025, at 1.5, 1.75 or 2.1 percent depending on prior-year income. A small business credit of up to $55 a month, or $160 for service-heavy businesses, offsets the tax at low volumes.
  • Sales tax, and this is the big one. Unlike Oregon, Washington taxes construction labor. On custom construction, "Prime contractors must collect retail sales tax from the landowner on the gross contract price," and the taxable amount "includes charges for permits and other fees, labor, profit, materials and charges for subcontractors" (Department of Revenue). Subcontractors on custom jobs take a reseller permit from the prime instead. The rate follows the job site, not your office.
  • Workers' compensation is state-run. "Washington State does not allow private workers' compensation coverage. You must purchase your coverage from L&I or be a certified self-insured employer" (L&I). Premiums are charged per hour worked rather than as a share of payroll, employees may pay part of the premium by law, and owners are "generally excluded from mandatory workers' compensation coverage" unless they elect it.
  • Paid leave programs. Paid Family and Medical Leave is 1.13 percent of wages in 2026, split 28.57 percent employer and 71.43 percent employee, and businesses with fewer than 50 employees "are not required to pay the employer portion" (ESD). WA Cares is 0.58 percent, paid entirely by the employee (WA Cares).
  • Local licenses. Seattle, for example, requires a business license tax certificate, with a 2026 fee of $73 for businesses under $20,000 in city revenue and an exemption at $4,000 for businesses without a location in the city (Seattle).

Step 9: Stay compliant

  • Renewals. The contractor license runs 24 months. Your electrician certificate runs three years with its 24 hours of continuing education. The master or administrator certificate renews every three years on the holder's birthday.
  • Keep the designation filled. If your master or administrator leaves, you have ten days to notify L&I, and the license depends on having one.
  • Ratios on every job site. They're the most commonly penalized thing on this list.
  • Prevailing wage. On public works you file a Statement of Intent to Pay Prevailing Wages and later an Affidavit of Wages Paid, each carrying a $40 filing fee (WAC 296-127-040 and WAC 296-127-045). Projects of $5,000 or less can use a combined form with no filing fee, and an agency may not "subdivide any public works project of more than $5,000 for the purpose of circumventing the procedures" (RCW 39.12.040).

What happens if you skip a license

L&I publishes a penalty schedule (WAC 296-46B-915):

  • Performing electrical work without a valid certificate, or outside its scope: $250 first offense, $500 after that.
  • Employing someone who doesn't hold a certificate or training certificate: $250, then $500.
  • Improper ratios, or failing to supervise a trainee: $250, then $500. Three ratio violations in three years and "Contractors found to have violated this section three times in a three-year period must be the subject of an electrical audit."
  • A trainee working unsupervised: $50, then $250, then $500.
  • Contracting without the electrical contractor license: $1,000, then $2,000, then $3,000, then $10,000 for each offense after that.
  • L&I "may double the penalty amount, up to ten thousand dollars."

The statute behind the schedule sets a range of "not less than $50 or more than $500" per violation and makes each day a separate violation (RCW 19.28.271). There's also a practical consequence that outlasts any fine: equipment installed by someone without a certificate "shall not receive an electrical work permit and electrical service shall not be connected or maintained to operate the equipment."

Frequently asked questions

Do you need a license to do electrical work in Washington?

Yes. Everyone learning the trade needs an electrical training certificate, and doing the work on your own requires an electrician certificate of competency. The business needs a separate electrical contractor license from L&I.

How many hours does it take to become a journey level electrician in Washington?

The general journey level (01) certificate requires 8,000 hours, of which 4,000 must be new industrial or commercial installations, and no more than 4,000 can come from specialties. You also need 96 hours of classroom instruction before you can sit the exam.

Can you still become an 01 electrician without an apprenticeship in Washington?

No. The route that allowed trainees to gain general journey level hours outside a registered apprenticeship closed on July 1, 2026. Specialty work is different: trainees do not need to be registered apprentices for it.

How many trainees can one electrician supervise in Washington?

One trainee per journey level electrician doing journey level work, and two per specialty electrician doing specialty work. The certified electrician has to be on the same job site at least 75 percent of each working day, and with three or four trainees it becomes 100 percent and the supervisor can't do installation work.

How much do electricians make in Washington?

The median wage for Washington electricians was $95,220 a year in May 2025 and the mean was $94,470, according to BLS. Only Oregon has a higher state mean.

Does Washington have electrician license reciprocity?

With Oregon only, and only for the general journey level (01) certificate. You need an Oregon J or S license obtained by Oregon examination, and you can use reciprocity in Washington only once.

What does it cost to start an electrical contracting business in Washington?

The state fees are $353.90 for the electrical contractor license ($306.20 online), $50 for the business license application, and $180 for an LLC. You also need a $4,000 surety bond and a workers' compensation account with L&I.

Does an electrical contractor need contractor registration in Washington?

Not for electrical work alone, which is exempt under RCW 18.27.090. If the business also does non-electrical work, registration is required, with a $15,000 bond for a specialty contractor or $30,000 for a general contractor.
